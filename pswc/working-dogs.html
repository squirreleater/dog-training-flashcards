Working Dogs

Dogs with a job

A working dog is not a pet with extra obedience. It is a dog selected, conditioned and maintained for a task — and the difference shows up on the worst day, not the best one.

Selection comes first

Most working-dog failures are selection failures wearing a training costume. A dog that will not hunt independently, cannot recover from a startle, or shuts down on a novel surface is not going to be trained out of it in eight weeks — and pretending otherwise wastes the handler's time and the dog's welfare.

PSWC assesses prospects on the traits that actually predict working success: hunt drive and persistence, environmental resilience, recovery time after a startle, social neutrality, and the ability to keep working while aroused rather than despite it.

Then conditioning

Once a dog is selected, the work is unglamorous. Odour is imprinted cleanly and paid at source. Search patterns are built so the handler can read them. Environments are added one variable at a time — surface, height, noise, light, people — and never all at once, because a dog that is overwhelmed is not learning anything you want it to keep.

Duration is built last. A dog that can search for forty minutes but has never been asked to commit to source under distraction is a dog that will quit at minute six in a real environment.

Deployment decisions

The dog is usually not the limiting factor. Handlers are trained to read change of behaviour, manage their own leash pressure, and know when to call it.

Standards

Trained to a standard, not to a timeline

A working dog is certified against a task, not a calendar. PSWC will say when a dog is not ready, and will say when a dog is not suitable. That conversation is cheaper than the alternative.
