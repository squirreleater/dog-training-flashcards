Building Engagement in an 11-Week-Old Working Puppy

An eleven-week-old working puppy arrives with a problem you cannot see yet: everything in the world is more interesting than you are. That is not a defect. It is a puppy doing exactly what a puppy is built to do. But if it is still true at eight months, you do not have a training problem — you have a relationship problem that training will not fix.

Engagement is the fix, and it has to be built now.

What engagement actually is

Engagement is the puppy voluntarily choosing to interact with you when it has other options. That is the whole definition, and the last clause is the important one. A puppy that works beautifully in a closed kitchen is not engaged; it is bored and you are the only game available.

It is worth being clear about what engagement is not:

  • It is not enthusiasm. A puppy can be wildly excited and completely unavailable to you.
  • It is not obedience. A puppy can sit on cue and still be scanning the treeline.
  • It is not eye contact. Eye contact is one behaviour that often accompanies engagement, and it is very easy to train in isolation as a hollow trick.

The test is simple. Take the puppy somewhere mildly interesting, do nothing, and count how long it takes before it looks at you. If you have to make a noise, you have your answer.

Engagement is what the puppy does when you have not asked for anything.

Start in a boring room

The first sessions happen somewhere with nothing in it. Not because that is where engagement is needed, but because that is where it can be paid cleanly.

Bring food the puppy actually wants — not kibble, unless the puppy is genuinely mad for kibble. Sit down, say nothing, and wait. The moment the puppy looks at you, mark it and pay it. Then wait again.

What you are building is a specific piece of information: looking at the handler produces good outcomes, reliably, and nothing else has to happen first. No cue, no name, no kissy noise. The moment you prompt it, you have taught the puppy that engagement is your job to start.

Mechanics that matter more than they should

  • Mark the decision, not the arrival. The mark goes on the head turn, not on the puppy reaching you.
  • Pay away from yourself sometimes. Throwing the reward resets the puppy to a distance, which means it has to choose you again. A puppy fed at your feet for thirty seconds has made one choice; a puppy fed at three metres has made ten.
  • End before it ends. Two minutes. Then put the food away while the puppy still wants more. Every session should finish with the puppy trying to continue.

Adding the world, one piece at a time

Once the puppy offers engagement in a dead room, the environment gets one increment harder. Not the car park at school pickup — the hallway. Then the garden. Then the garden with somebody walking past at forty metres.

At each step, expect the behaviour to get worse. That is not failure, that is the point of the exercise: you are finding out whether the behaviour generalises, and it never does for free. If the puppy cannot offer engagement at a given distance, you are too close. Move back until it can, pay it heavily, and leave.

The mistake almost everyone makes is holding criteria constant while the environment changes. If you have moved somewhere harder, lower what you are asking for. A glance is a win in a car park.

Toy mechanics

Food builds engagement. Toys build intensity. Working puppies need both, and toy work introduces a problem food does not have: the puppy can win the toy and leave.

So the toy is not a possession, it is an interaction. Short, two-handed tug with clear starts and clear stops. The puppy wins regularly — a puppy that never wins stops playing — but the game restarts because the handler makes it interesting again, not because the puppy is chased.

If the puppy takes the toy and goes to a corner with it, the toy is more valuable than you are. Go back to food, build the pattern, and reintroduce the toy on a line.

What this looks like at six months

You will not see a dog performing tricks. You will see a dog that comes out of the crate and immediately looks for its handler. You will see a dog that can be walked past a distraction and voluntarily checks in. You will see a dog that, when something startles it, orients to the handler rather than away.

That last one is the payoff. Every difficult thing you will ask this dog to do later — environmental work, drive work, protection work — depends on the dog treating the handler as the thing that makes sense of a confusing situation. You are not building a behaviour. You are building the default.
