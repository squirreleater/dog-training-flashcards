Sport Foundations Before Formal Obedience

There is a strong pull, with a young dog that is doing well, to start naming things. The puppy is offering a sit; call it "sit". The puppy is following the food into position; call it "heel". It feels like progress, and the video looks good.

It is usually a mistake, and the cost does not show up for months.

A cue locks in whatever it is attached to

When you put a cue on a behaviour, you are not labelling the behaviour you intend. You are labelling the behaviour the dog is actually performing, in the position it is actually in, with whatever errors it currently contains.

Name a crooked sit and you have trained a crooked sit. Name a heel position the dog is drifting out of and you have trained drift. Removing that later is harder than teaching it clean, because you are now competing with a cued, reinforced history rather than starting from nothing.

Teach the behaviour to a standard. Then name it. Never the other way round.

What foundations means in practice

Before any formal obedience is cued, a sport prospect should have a handful of things that are not exercises at all:

  • Engagement. The dog offers interaction in progressively harder environments without being asked.
  • A conditioned marker. One sound, one meaning, delivered with consistent timing. The marker is the whole communication system; if it is sloppy, everything downstream is sloppy.
  • Reward mechanics. The dog takes food cleanly, tugs on rules, outs on a cue that has been built rather than fought for, and can be reset to distance.
  • Position understanding. The dog knows where its body goes — front position, heel position, platform work — shaped and paid, with no verbal attached.
  • Rear-end awareness. Unglamorous and enormously useful. A dog that does not know where its back feet are cannot hold precise positions under distraction.

None of that is impressive to watch. All of it determines the ceiling.

Precision is built before duration, duration before distraction

The order matters and it is routinely inverted. Handlers add duration to a sloppy position because duration is easy to see improving, then add distraction to a position that was never right, then wonder why the exercise falls apart at a trial.

Get the shape right at one second. Then hold the shape for ten. Then hold it while something happens. If the shape degrades when you add a variable, remove the variable — you have found the edge of the dog's understanding, which is exactly the information you wanted.

Splitting exercises

A finished sport routine is a chain. Chains are trained backwards and in pieces, never as a whole. The retrieve is not one exercise — it is the hold, the carry, the out, the front, the send, and the pickup, each of which is trained separately to a standard before any two are joined.

Handlers who train the chain rehearse the weakest link every single repetition. Handlers who split get to work on the weak link in isolation, which is the only place it can actually be fixed.

The cue goes on last, and on purpose

When the behaviour is reliable, clean, and offered without prompting, the verbal goes on. Say it just before the dog does the thing it was going to do anyway, several dozen times, then start testing whether the word produces it.

Introduced this way, the cue means something specific from the first repetition, and the dog is never in the position of guessing what a word refers to. That guessing period — where the word is being said over a behaviour that is still forming — is where most cue problems are born.

This costs time you will get back

Foundation work is slow, it does not photograph well, and clients ask when the real training starts. The honest answer is that this is the real training, and the visible part later goes roughly four times faster because of it.

The dogs that stall at eighteen months — the ones that look good in the club field and come apart at a trial — are almost never dogs that lacked ability. They are dogs whose foundations were skipped because the routine was more fun to train.
